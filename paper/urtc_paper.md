# Reading Redistricting Plans by Shape: A Graph Neural Network Complement to Vote-Based Outlier Tests

**Abstract** — Whether a districting plan was drawn to favor a party is judged by comparing it against a large ensemble of neutral plans. That comparison almost always runs through a summary of the vote distribution, whose reading shifts with the election chosen and fades as a state leans toward one party. We train a graph neural network to make the comparison on a plan's shape instead, scoring how far an enacted map's geometry departs from the neutral ensemble without reading a single vote. Because settlement density shadows both geometry and partisanship, we plant packing and cracking edits at density-correlated and density-neutral locations and ask whether the detector reads shape or shortcuts through the demographic pattern. It reads shape: it recovers the moved precincts where density cannot mark them, and its per-precinct localization beats a hand-built boundary or cut-edge score, held to the same ensemble comparison, on two of the three states. On North Carolina, Pennsylvania, and Maryland the geometric reading and the vote statistics are complementary, geometry catching the plans whose gerrymander bends the district lines and staying silent on the one whose gerrymander lives in the votes. Reading a plan's shape and summarizing its votes are two measurements of one object, better used together than apart.

**Keywords** — gerrymandering, redistricting, graph neural networks, anomaly detection, algorithmic auditing

## I. Introduction

Pennsylvania's congressional map of 2011 carried a district so contorted that observers named it Goofy Kicking Donald Duck, and Maryland drew a district of the same decade whose outline earned it the nickname the praying mantis. Both maps were later challenged as partisan gerrymanders. So was North Carolina's, drawn to the same end with districts that look, on the page, largely unremarkable. Partisan intent, then, is not something a plan's shape always betrays, and it is not something the plan announces.

To settle the question, analysts compare the enacted plan against a large ensemble of alternative plans drawn under neutral criteria and ask whether the enacted plan is an outlier [1], [2]. Courts have relied on the comparison, and the Markov-chain samplers that produce the ensembles are now standard [1], [3]. What the enacted plan is scored on, though, is almost always a single summary of the vote distribution, usually the mean-median difference or the efficiency gap, and those summaries are contested: they disagree with one another, they move with the choice of election, and their meaning erodes as a state's balance tilts toward one party [6], [7], [8].

We keep the ensemble comparison but change what is compared. Rather than summarize how a plan splits the vote, a graph neural network learns what the neutral ensemble's plans look like as shapes over the precinct adjacency graph and scores an enacted plan by how far its geometry departs from them. Because the network never reads an election return, its verdict cannot depend on which election is chosen, which is precisely where the vote summaries are least dependable.

A learned detector invites an obvious suspicion. Settlement density shapes both where a party's voters live and the terrain a plan must cut through, so a network handed demographic features might appear to detect gerrymandering while in fact keying on a demographic shortcut [14], [15]. We take that suspicion seriously enough to build the experiment around it, planting the two moves a partisan gerrymander is built from, cracking a bloc of opposition voters across many districts and packing it into a few, at density-correlated and at density-neutral locations, then asking whether the detector still finds the moved precincts once the demographic pattern no longer marks them.

The detector passes that test. On the density-neutral ground where the demographic pattern gives the moved precincts nothing to hide behind, the topology-only variant recovers them from their geometry alone, while adding the demographic features only invites a vote-share shortcut. This confound-isolation result anchors the paper, and the rest of its contribution gathers around it. No learned graph-anomaly detector has to our knowledge been brought to redistricting outlier detection, a setting where ensemble analysis has relied on fixed statistics and the nearest alternatives either measure distances between plans or cluster them without reading votes or demographics [20], [22]. Across all three states, in turn, the geometric and the vote-based tests turn out complementary, their disagreement tracing to a single mechanism by which the network reads the cracking that bends a district's outline and stays blind to the packing a compact district can conceal.

## II. Background and Related Work

Ensemble outlier analysis compares an enacted plan against a neutral reference distribution built by a Markov chain over graph partitions [1]. We read the plan's position as a percentile of that distribution rather than as the Chikina-Frieze-Pegden certificate, which needs no mixing but applies only to a walk started from the enacted plan itself [2]. Developed on North Carolina, the method has since been used in litigation [3].

Almost always the plan is scored on a partisan-asymmetry statistic, and almost always that statistic is the mean-median difference or the efficiency gap [9]. Both collapse the vote distribution to a single number, and both are contested. Reallocating votes among a party's own districts leaves the mean-median difference unmoved so long as the median district does not change [4]; the efficiency gap conflates bias with disproportionality [9]; and both inherit their meaning from a uniform-swing counterfactual whose assumptions frequently fail [10]. DeFord and Veomett prove the sharper version of the problem, that as a state's vote leaves parity the range these statistics can take collapses, so a lopsided state can hide an extreme seat outcome from them entirely [7], a failure their ensemble study confirms empirically [8]. Declination, which compares each party's average winning margin against its own seat share, escapes the worst of this [5].

Graph-level anomaly detection is by now a developed area of machine learning, though not one that has been turned on redistricting. We build on glocal knowledge distillation, in which a student network is trained to match a frozen random teacher at both the node and the graph level and a graph is scored by their disagreement [11], on the graph isomorphism network for its message-passing expressiveness [12], and on the wider anomaly-detection literature [13]. Within redistricting the nearest work is a hand-built geometric distance between plans [20] and an unsupervised clustering that reads no vote or demographic data and returns a consensus-plan visualization rather than a per-plan outlier score [22]; neither learns a representation nor tests for the shortcut, and the one study that localizes manipulated precincts does so by combinatorial search over a legal disenfranchisement score rather than by learning [21]. The confound our experiment targets is the political-geography effect by which residential density produces partisan bias even under neutral lines [14], and the shortcut it invites is a familiar failure mode in which a model exploits a spurious feature in place of the signal it was meant to capture [15].

## III. A Learned Geometric Detector

A state's precinct adjacency graph, its dual graph, is fixed across every plan: precincts are the nodes, and an edge joins two precincts that share a border. What changes from one plan to the next is the assignment of precincts to districts, and with it both the features each precinct carries and which edges are cut, meaning those that join two precincts placed in different districts (Fig. 1). From an assignment we compute per-precinct geometry: whether a precinct lies on a district boundary, how much of its boundary faces other districts, a compactness ratio, its share of its district's population, and fixed attributes of its shape.

[[FIG_TOY]]

Our detector, drawn in Fig. 2, follows the glocal construction of Ma et al. [11]. Because a state's precinct graph is shared by every plan, distinguishing plans here means detecting an anomalous configuration of node features on one fixed graph rather than comparing graphs of different shapes, the setting that construction was built for; the reduction is deliberate and is what lets a single precomputed adjacency serve the whole ensemble. A graph isomorphism network with random, frozen weights acts as a teacher, and a second network of the same shape is trained to reproduce the teacher's node and graph embeddings across the neutral ensemble. Since the ensemble is all the student ever sees, it learns to imitate the teacher only on typical neutral plans, and a plan on which the two networks part ways is anomalous by construction. We summarize the per-node disagreement into a plan score using three standardized moments of its distribution, its mean, its standard deviation, and its maximum, rather than the mean alone, a precaution against a weakness of mean pooling documented for reconstruction-based graph scorers [23], and we read outlier status from the plan's two-sided tail position among the ensemble's scores. The student is a three-layer graph isomorphism network of sixty-four hidden units, trained for up to two hundred epochs by Adam at a learning rate of 0.001 with early stopping on a held-out split, and every localization and outlier number we report averages three independent training seeds. The enacted plan enters none of this training, the detector is fit on neutral plans, and the outlier threshold is the ninety-ninth percentile the field already uses, so neither the model nor the cutoff is tuned to the maps we go on to test.

To probe the confound we run the detector in two feature variants. One is topology-only, drawing on the geometry above and nothing else. The other appends the three covariates through which a shortcut would run, precinct population density, minority voting-age-population share, and Democratic vote share.

## IV. Confound Isolation and Data

To test whether the detector localizes an actual manipulation, we plant packing and cracking edits into neutral plans and ask each variant to recover the precincts that moved. A planted edit slides a contiguous strip of precincts across a district line while keeping both districts contiguous and within the population bound. The two conditions differ only in where the strip is drawn. In the correlated condition it sits on dense, partisan-extreme ground, where a density shortcut would find it; in the decorrelated condition it sits on density-neutral ground, where the shortcut is useless and only the geometry of the edit remains. A detector that still localizes in the decorrelated condition is reading shape, not the confound. The planting behaves as designed: across the three states the decorrelated strips sit at a density z-score near zero, from −0.33 to −0.13, with only a small residual density-partisanship correlation, while the correlated strips sit well above average density, at a z-score of 0.35 to 3.2.

We evaluate on the enacted congressional plans of three states from the MGGG precinct shapefiles, chosen to span the regimes where the question behaves differently rather than for convenience. North Carolina is close to balanced and Republican-drawn; Pennsylvania is close to balanced, Republican-drawn, and geometrically contorted; Maryland is lopsidedly Democratic, the regime where the vote statistics are known to struggle. For each state we build two independent recombination ensembles, one to train the detector and one to test it, so that no score is judged on the trajectory that produced it, and the enacted plan is held out as the positive control, entering neither chain. Each reference ensemble holds 467 neutral plans drawn along a six-thousand-step chain, and the planted-edit study moves one hundred and twenty strips per condition, split evenly between packing and cracking edits.

## V. The Detector on Three States

Fig. 3 answers the question the plan-level verdict cannot, whether the learned detector finds the moved precincts by more than the boundary structure a reader could compute by hand. We score each precinct by how far its learned embedding sits from that precinct's own distribution across the neutral ensemble, a conditional anomaly that fires when a precinct's configuration has become unusual for it, and we hold the hand-built geometry to the same ensemble comparison, ranking a precinct by its boundary exposure and its incident cut edges, each measured against its own neutral distribution. On North Carolina and Pennsylvania the learned score clears the hand baseline under both planting conditions, reaching an area under the curve of 0.85 to 0.95 against 0.79 to 0.88, a margin of four to seven points. On Maryland the two run even, near 0.79 on the decorrelated strips and 0.85 on the correlated ones, with a raw boundary score one to five points ahead. The learned representation carries localizing signal a hand statistic misses on two of the three states, for a reason visible in the edit itself: a planted strip is coordinated and contiguous, so a precinct in its interior keeps an ordinary boundary while its neighborhood has changed districts, a shift the embedding registers where a lone boundary number cannot.

[[FIG_LOCAL]]

Two checks locate and bound that gain. A frozen random network of the same shape, scored the same way, localizes just as well, so the gain is the conditioned message-passing representation rather than the training, which instead powers the plan-level outlier score through the teacher-student divergence. And the detector reads shape rather than the demographic confound: it localizes on the decorrelated strips, where density carries no information about which precincts moved, and its plan score is by construction invariant to a shuffle of the vote-share labels, a shuffle that leaves the topology-only score exactly unchanged while moving the demographic variant's by twenty to seventy-two times the spread of its own neutral ensemble. One qualification tempers the isolation claim. The topology features are not fully independent of density by design, since a precinct's area enters both its shape and, through population over area, its density, so what the topology-only variant supplies is not architectural independence but the behavioral evidence above. Plant-level standard deviations run near 0.10, but the scores are read on the same edits, so the paired margin on North Carolina and Pennsylvania is real while Maryland's sits at zero. These are area-under-curve comparisons, and the absolute precision at the true number of moved precincts is small, so the localization stands as evidence that the learned representation carries geometric signal a hand statistic misses rather than as a precinct-level auditing tool.

With the detector's reading of geometry established, we turn it on the three enacted plans. Table I gives each plan's percentile against its ensemble on five detectors under the 2016 presidential vote, and a geometry-versus-vote division runs down it. The geometry detectors, the cut-edge count and the learned score, single out Pennsylvania and Maryland and miss North Carolina; the vote statistics catch North Carolina, and among them the efficiency gap and declination also reach the threshold on Maryland while none reaches it on Pennsylvania. North Carolina is caught only by the votes and Pennsylvania only by the geometry, the two ends of the complementarity, and the mean-median difference, singled out below, is the one vote statistic that misses Maryland as well.

[[TABLE_I]]

One might read Table I as showing the learned score doing no more than rediscovering the cut-edge count, and at the plan level that reading is largely fair: the two geometry rows move together, because both answer to the boundary structure a message-passing network is built to see [16], [17]. We show the agreement rather than hide it. The network's contribution is not a different plan-level verdict but the node-level localization of Fig. 3, which matches the boundary and cut-edge scores a single number would supply and beats them outright on two of the three states, together with the evidence that the localization reads shape rather than the confound; what the cut-edge count offers in return is a plain reading of why the plan-level score behaves as it does.

That reason is visible directly. Fig. 4 places each enacted plan against the distribution of cut edges across its ensemble. North Carolina's plan sits near the median of its distribution, about as compact as a typical neutral plan, while Pennsylvania's and Maryland's fall far outside their ensembles, at roughly six and fourteen standard deviations. A second compactness measure, the Polsby-Popper score, points the same way: North Carolina's plan is more compact than its entire ensemble, as packing an already-clustered electorate would leave it, while Maryland's is far less compact, as cracking would. Recombination ensembles are themselves tilted toward compact plans, which for our purpose is convenient rather than troubling [1]: a plan carrying far more cut edges than a null already weighted toward compactness is an outlier against a conservative reference, not a lax one. The learned score calls a plan an outlier precisely when the plan's shape is anomalous, and North Carolina's is not.

Because the mean-median difference depends on the election, we recompute it under every statewide race each state offers (Table II); its verdict will not hold still, catching North Carolina under all five races, Pennsylvania under four of nine, and Maryland under none, the pattern the achievable-range bound predicts as balance leaves parity [7]. The learned geometric score, reading no votes, returns one verdict per state that no election can disturb.

[[TABLE_II]]

The geometric verdicts belong to the plans, not to the chain that drew them. Rebuilding every state's chain at half and at double its length and at population-balance tolerances of one and five percent moves no cut-edge verdict across its threshold under any of the five constructions, with North Carolina inside the body of its distribution and Pennsylvania and Maryland at the ensemble maximum in every case. The vote statistics hold less firmly, as North Carolina's mean-median difference slips to the ninety-sixth percentile at the five percent tolerance and Pennsylvania's efficiency gap crosses the threshold on the half-length chain. Because the learned score answers to cut edges at the plan level, its geometric verdict should inherit this stability.

## VI. The Packing–Cracking Mechanism

One distinction runs through the whole pattern. Cracking bends boundaries and cuts adjacencies, whereas packing an opposition that already clusters in cities needs almost no bending at all, so a detector that reads only shape sees the first and stays blind to the second. Pennsylvania's and Maryland's bent boundaries put them in the geometric tail, consistent with an advantage built by cracking, whereas North Carolina's ordinary shape is consistent with one built by packing an already-clustered electorate, and the shape detector reads each accordingly. A compact plan can be a severe gerrymander [18], and compactness has long been known to be neither necessary nor sufficient for detecting one [19]. The North Carolina miss is not a defect to be explained away, then, but one sense in which a geometric test and a vote-based test measure different things.

The vote side of the disagreement has its own account. Maryland is the shape the mean-median difference is built to miss, a wall of comfortable but not overwhelming Democratic seats that never disturbs the median district [4], [6], and because the statistic's power drains away as the vote leaves parity, its silence there is structural rather than bad luck [7]. Declination, which sets each party's average winning margin against its own seat share rather than a raw share against a fixed threshold, is built to survive that lopsided baseline, and across the multi-election sweep of Table II it recovers Maryland under four of its six races where the mean-median difference recovers none [5]. Two families of tests read two signatures of the same act, and a panel that reports both says more than either alone.

The demographic variant's collapse is the shortcut the planted-edit study was built to expose. Training prefers the simplest feature that predicts and stays blind to more complex structure that predicts as well [24], so once vote share is on offer the network leans on it in place of geometry, which is why the augmented variant wobbles under a label shuffle. Keeping the features to geometry is what keeps the detector reading shape.

## VII. Limitations and Availability

The detector complements the vote-based tests without replacing them, and the complement is also the limit: a packing gerrymander with compact districts is invisible to shape, and only the votes will find it. Our evidence covers three states and one election vintage, and the planted-edit study tests localization on manipulations of enacted geography rather than on an independent collection of maps, though the three enacted plans were each litigated as partisan gerrymanders. The percentiles of Table I are descriptive positions rather than a pooled test, and with 467 plans no two-sided tail can fall below 2/468, so we make no formal claim across its fifteen cells and read the learned-score calls for Pennsylvania and Maryland as borderline. Our ensembles are recombination chains rather than any state's own legal reference distribution, so the percentiles report position against a standard neutral null, and no method here, election-based ones included, escapes the underlying difficulty that a map close to the ensemble can still be unfair [20]. Pooling the geometric and vote-based families under a formal multiple-comparison correction would turn the panel we advocate into one calibrated decision rather than a reader's judgment across separate columns, and the confound-checked node localization could serve as the learned front end for the per-precinct disenfranchisement audits that current work reaches only by combinatorial search over a fixed score [21]. The code and state configurations are available at github.com/human-vc/redistricting-gnn-confound, so that the geometric score can be read beside the classical statistics for any state.

## VIII. Conclusion

We trained a graph neural network to find redistricting outliers from the shape of a plan, gathered planted-edit evidence that it localizes manipulated precincts by geometry rather than by a demographic shortcut, and ran it on the enacted congressional maps of three states. It singles out the two maps whose gerrymander is cut into their district lines and that the mean-median difference misses or reads inconsistently, and it stays silent on the map whose gerrymander is hidden in the votes. The split is not an accident of these three maps. It follows from a distinction that should hold wherever district lines are drawn: a gerrymander built by cracking leaves a geometric trace while one built by packing an already-clustered electorate does not, so we expect a shape-reading detector and a vote-reading statistic to keep disagreeing in this patterned way beyond the three maps examined here, a prediction a broader panel of states could test. What that gives an analyst is a second reading, one that owes nothing to the choice of election, to set beside the mean-median difference, the efficiency gap, and declination in place of asking any single number to carry the judgment. Reading a plan's shape and summarizing its votes are two measurements of one object, and telling whether a map is fair is better done with both in hand than with either alone.
